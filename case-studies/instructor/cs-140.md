## Reasoning walkthrough

**Worked instance.** Two bookshops' bars with per-customer revenue and
customer mixes; the newspaper reads the blend and crowns South.

**Expected result.** demo prints `blended: North 6.67 vs South 13.5`
then `sliced: books 30 vs 16, coffee 2 vs 1 — North leads within both
bars` — the blend reverses the per-bar comparison because South's
mix is dominated by its high-revenue books bar (100 vs 20
customers), while North's is dominated by its low-revenue coffee
bar.

**Method — aggregation, bias, data.** Walk the class through these
moves:

1. Compute the blends honestly: North (20×30 + 100×2)/120 = 6.67;
   South (100×16 + 20×1)/120 = 13.50. The newspaper's arithmetic is
   *correct* — that is what makes the case worth teaching.
2. Slice by bar: books 30 vs 16; coffee 2 vs 1. Every sub-group
   favours North; the aggregate favours South. Write both rows before
   explaining anything.
3. Explain the flip with the weights: a blended average is a
   weighted average whose weights are the group sizes; South puts
   83% of its customers in its high-revenue bar, North puts 83% in
   its low-revenue bar. The mix, not the bars, drives the blend.
4. The fair comparison: report each bar per customer, or recompute
   both cities under one fixed (standardised) mix — either way, print
   the weights next to every blend you publish.

**Where this class gets stuck.** Students recompute the blend three
times looking for an error — there is none; that dead end is the
lesson. Second stall: "so aggregates are useless" — push back: the
aggregate answers a real question (who earns more per visitor?) that
the slices cannot; the failure is quoting it as "better business"
without its weights.

## Five-minute teaching sequence

Minute 0–1 — **Read and analyse.** Expect both blends computed. Watch
for: averaging the two bars' revenues equally (30+2)/2.
Minute 1–3 — **Develop an approach.** Expect the slice table; a
correct blend pair with slices missing is partial credit. Watch for:
comparing 6.67 vs 13.50 and stopping at "South wins".
Minute 3–5 — **Compare and write.** Expect the two-sentence
explanation and the newspaper comparison drafted.
Reveal and discuss — keep the reveal inside 10 minutes so the exit question still fits.

## Difficulty adaptation

- **Simplify.** Give the two blends and the slice table; ask only for
  the explanation of the flip.
- **Extend.** Ask for a standardised mix (say 50/50 customers per
  bar) and recompute: (30+2)/2 = 16 vs (16+1)/2 = 8.5 — North leads
  under any fixed mix; discuss what standardisation claims and hides.
- **Hints (progressive).**
  1. *Re-orient:* "Compute each shop's total revenue and total
     customers before averaging."
  2. *Structure:* "Blend, slice, then explain with the weights."
  3. *Near-answer:* the common wrong turn is an unweighted bar
     average — undo that choice and re-derive the step it breaks.

## Reference solution (pseudocode)

```
blended(shop) <- sum(rev[bar] * mix[bar]) / sum(mix[bar])
slice         <- rev[bar] per bar, side by side
explanation   <- blend is a weighted average; weights are the mix
fair          <- report slices with the blend, or fix the mix
```

## Reference solution (Python)

```python
REV = {"North": {"books": 30, "coffee": 2},
       "South": {"books": 16, "coffee": 1}}
MIX = {"North": {"books": 20, "coffee": 100},
       "South": {"books": 100, "coffee": 20}}

def blended(shop):
    tot = sum(REV[shop][b] * MIX[shop][b] for b in REV[shop])
    n = sum(MIX[shop].values())
    return tot / n

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("blended:", {s: round(blended(s), 2) for s in REV})
    print("sliced: books", REV["North"]["books"], "vs",
          REV["South"]["books"],
          "| coffee", REV["North"]["coffee"], "vs",
          REV["South"]["coffee"])
```

The code is runnable as-is; executing the file prints the worked instance
so you can demo it live on the projector.

## Edge cases

- Equal mixes (50/50 both cities) — blend order matches slice order;
  the paradox needs *unequal, opposing* weights to appear.
- A bar with zero customers — its revenue drops out of the blend and
  the slice; the denominator story must say so.
- Three bars instead of two — the same fold; the explanation does not
  change.

## Alternative approaches

- Standardised mix (fixed weights) — the cleanest headline number;
  costs you the cities' real economics, so print both.
- Report totals (revenue × customers) instead of per-customer —
  different question again; North's total? 20×30+100×2 = 800 vs
  South 100×16+20×1 = 1620 — South wins on *total* too, which is
  worth saying: the paradox is about per-customer claims.
- Refuse aggregates entirely — overreaction; the discipline is
  weights-with-blend, not no-blend.
- **Cost.** All variants are constant arithmetic at this size; the
  real cost of the error is a wrong procurement decision reported in
  a newspaper.

## Common pitfalls

- Averaging the two bars' revenues without weights.
- Believing the blend is an error and recomputing forever.
- Reporting the slice table without the blend (or vice versa).
- Explaining the flip without naming the weights.

## Reveal script

1. Display the case and start the timer (5 minutes). While students work,
   circulate and read shoulders; pick one conventional attempt and one
   surprising one for the board.
2. Ask first: "Before any averaging — what are the weights?"
3. Compute both blends on the board, then the slice table beneath.
4. Surface the wrong turn directly: the unweighted average — compute
  (30+2)/2 = 16 vs (16+1)/2 = 8.5 and compare with the blends.
5. Show the reference Python, run it on the worked instance, and let the
   class compare with their own answers.
6. Close by naming the technique — "weighted averages and the mix
   behind them" — and hand over the discussion prompt about the
   single number.

## Assessment use

- **Formative use.** discussion-grade: mark weight-awareness and the
  fairness of the proposed comparison.
- **Mini-rubric (10 points).**
  - *Structure (2):* blends computed as weighted averages.
  - *Method (3):* slice table complete; flip explained by the mix.
  - *Result (3):* 6.67 vs 13.50 blended; 30/16 and 2/1 sliced.
  - *Communication (2):* newspaper comparison prints its weights.
- **Follow-up check.** South's coffee bar doubles its price (1 → 2):
  which number moves, and does the headline flip back?
