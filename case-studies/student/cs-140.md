## Problem

Two campus bookshops, each with a coffee bar. Per-shop revenue per
customer:

```
           Books  Coffee
North         30      2
South         16      1
```

Per-bookshop customer mix:

```
           Books-bar  Coffee-bar customers
North           20                100
South          100                 20
```

The city newspaper writes: "South's bookshops run a more lucrative
business — look at their blended revenue per customer." Compute the
blended figure and you will see their point. Then:

Your deliverables:

1. The blended revenue per customer for each bookshop.
2. The per-bar comparison (books bar vs books bar, coffee bar vs coffee
   bar) that the blend hides.
3. In two sentences: what happened, and why "who buys what" can flip an
   aggregate while every sub-group ranking stays the same.
4. The fair two-line comparison you would give the newspaper instead.

If the problem is ambiguous anywhere, state the assumption you are making
and carry on — a stated assumption is part of a good answer.

## Examples

- **Input:** the per-bar revenue table and the customer-mix table
- **Output:** North blended 6.67 vs South blended 13.50 — yet North
  leads *within* both bars — plus the explanation and the fair comparison

Solve for the worked instance first. Then ask what would change if one
number, name, or condition were replaced by something stranger.

## What to notice

- A blend is a **weighted** average, and the weights are the group
  sizes — change who buys what and the blend moves even when no bar
  gets better.
- The paradox is only visible when you *slice*: aggregate, then break
  apart by the same grouping that drives the weights.
- The remedy is not "never aggregate" — it is reporting the aggregate
  **with** the weights, or a like-for-like slice.

## Discussion starter

After the solution is revealed, the class spends five minutes on this
prompt: **"The newspaper insists on one number per city. What single
number would you defend, computed how — and what must you print next to
it?"**

## Constraints and assumptions

- Difficulty: Intermediate (aggregate reasoning with a twist).
- Topics: aggregation, bias, data.
- Revenue figures are per customer per bar; mixes are exact customer
  counts.
- Classroom-scale inputs unless the problem says otherwise.
- Time-box: about five minutes for a first attempt; partial answers with
  clear reasoning are worth more than silent struggling.
- Optional stretch (not required): describe an alternative approach and
  say when it beats the obvious one.

## Hints and extension

- First nudge: compute each shop's total revenue and total customers
  before averaging anything.
- If stuck after three minutes, write the input/output line you expect and
  trace the smallest example by hand.
- After the reveal, attempt the extension below before the next lecture.
- Extension challenge: design a *standardised* mix (fixed weights) and
  recompute both cities under it — what claim does the standardisation
  let you make, and what does it hide?
