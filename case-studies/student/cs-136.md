## Problem

Four study groups — G1, G2, G3, G4 — must book presentation slots. Some
pairs share members and cannot present in the same slot:

```
conflicts: G1–G2   G1–G3   G2–G3   G2–G4   G3–G4
```

Your deliverables:

1. The **model**: draw the conflict graph (who is an edge?).
2. A **lower-bound argument**: why can two slots never suffice? (Point
   at the specific structure, don't just say "it feels tight".)
3. A three-slot timetable that satisfies every conflict.
4. The trade-off sentence: is "fewest slots" always the right objective?
   Name one stakeholder for whom it is not.

If the problem is ambiguous anywhere, state the assumption you are making
and carry on — a stated assumption is part of a good answer.

## Examples

- **Input:** the five conflict pairs
- **Output:** minimum 3 slots — e.g. slot 1: G1, G4; slot 2: G2;
  slot 3: G3 — plus the lower-bound argument

Solve for the worked instance first. Then ask what would change if one
number, name, or condition were replaced by something stranger.

## What to notice

- The conflicts are a **graph**; slots are colours; the minimum number
  of slots is the chromatic number — the modelling step does most of the
  work.
- A lower bound comes from structure: three groups that pairwise
  conflict need three distinct slots, whatever else happens.
- Greedy first-fit colouring in a fixed order produces *a* valid answer
  quickly — but the order can change the count, which matters when the
  count is the objective.

## Discussion starter

After the solution is revealed, the class spends five minutes on this
prompt: **"A fifth group G5 arrives, conflicting only with G1. Does the
minimum stay at 3? Give the timetable or the new lower bound."**

## Constraints and assumptions

- Difficulty: Advanced (modelling plus an argument, not just an answer).
- Topics: graphs, scheduling, strategy.
- Slots are unbounded in capacity: any number of groups may share a slot
  as long as no conflicting pair does.
- Classroom-scale inputs unless the problem says otherwise.
- Time-box: about five minutes for a first attempt; partial answers with
  clear reasoning are worth more than silent struggling.
- Optional stretch (not required): describe an alternative approach and
  say when it beats the obvious one.

## Hints and extension

- First nudge: find three groups where *every pair* conflicts.
- If stuck after three minutes, write the input/output line you expect and
  trace the smallest example by hand.
- After the reveal, attempt the extension below before the next lecture.
- Extension challenge: find a conflict graph on these four groups whose
  greedy first-fit count depends on the order — and give the two orders.
