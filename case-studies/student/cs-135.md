## Problem

A team builds a study-hours → pass/fail predictor for 40 students and
reports: *"test accuracy computed on 20 held-out rows."* Their pipeline:

1. Standardise the feature: scaled = (hours − mean) / sd, with mean and
   sd computed **over all 40 rows**.
2. Rule: predict **pass** if scaled hours ≥ 0.
3. Evaluate on a random 20-row test split carved from the same 40.

Your deliverables:

1. In three sentences a non-technical dean would understand: why is the
   reported number optimistic?
2. The minimal **correct** pipeline order (what is computed where).
3. The worked demonstration below, by hand: one student whose pass/fail
   **decision flips** between the leaky and the correct pipeline.
4. One further leak in this setup, beyond the scaling one.

The eight-row teaching dataset (feature = study hours; label in
parentheses):

```
2 (fail)  4 (fail)  4 (fail)  6 (fail)
7 (pass)  10 (pass)  12 (pass)  14 (pass)
```

For the demonstration, take the test split to be the rows
`2, 7, 12, 14` and the training split the other four rows
(`4, 4, 6, 10`). Standardise the test row `7` twice — once with the
mean and sd computed over all eight rows (leaky), once over the four
training rows (correct) — and apply the rule each time.

If the problem is ambiguous anywhere, state the assumption you are making
and carry on — a stated assumption is part of a good answer.

## Examples

- **Input:** the eight-row dataset; the two standardisation choices
- **Output:** leaky: scaled −0.0945 → fail — correct: scaled +0.4082 →
  pass — the same student, two decisions

Solve for the worked instance first. Then ask what would change if one
number, name, or condition were replaced by something stranger.

## What to notice

- Scaling is not cosmetic: the rule's threshold lives in *scaled* space,
  so whoever computes the mean controls the verdict.
- The leak is information flow: eight rows include the test row, so the
  "unseen" case helped set its own yardstick.
- A decision flip is the strongest form of evidence — one traced student
  persuades a dean better than an abstract warning.

immaterial.' Draft your two-sentence reply, using the pipeline order

## Hints and extension

- First nudge: list the quantities the leaky pipeline computes from all
  rows before anything is "held out".
- If stuck after three minutes, write the input/output line you expect and
  trace the smallest example by hand.
- After the reveal, attempt the extension below before the next lecture.
- Extension challenge: with the same eight rows, choose a test split for
  which the two pipelines **agree** — and characterise (no exhaustive
  search needed) which test rows can never flip.
