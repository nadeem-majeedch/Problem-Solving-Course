## Definitions

- **Strategy** — a complete plan of action for every situation that might arise.
- **Dominant strategy** — one that is at least as good in every scenario - bid your true value in a second-price auction.
- **Information set** — what a decision-maker knows (and does not) at decision time; the host's knowledge changes everything.
- **Best response** — the optimal move given what others do - the one-level-up thinking the auction case trains.
- **Equilibrium** — a profile of strategies where nobody gains by changing alone; introduced informally.

## Explanation

**Strategy means a plan for every situation.** Rock-paper-scissors has no fixed best move; the auction has a dominant one. The distinction seeds the whole session.

**Second-price logic: bid your value.** Overbidding risks paying too much; underbidding only risks losing wins you did not want at that price. The truth-telling property is derived from a two-bidder payoff table the class fills in.

**The host's knowledge is the hidden assumption.** Monty opens a door he KNOWS hides a goat. If the door were opened at random, switching gains nothing. Same script, different information - different strategy.

**Best response as a habit.** The popup-quiz auction trains 'think one level up': predict others' reasoning, then choose. Students write the other side's thinking before their own move.

**Cheap talk vs commitment.** In the group-project game, promises are free andbreakable; binding commitments change the game. Recognising which game you are in is the workshop's exit skill.

## Common misconceptions

- Playing a fixed move in games that require unpredictability.
- Assuming everyone sees the same information you do - the host's knowledge changes the game.
- Bidding above value 'to be safe' - the second-price table shows the exact risk.
- Treating promises as commitments; only binding actions change incentives.

## Summary and key takeaways

1. A strategy is a plan for every situation, not one move.
2. Second-price auctions reward truthful bidding - derive, do not memorise.
3. Information asymmetry changes optimal play.
4. Think one level up: model the other side before moving.

## Practice questions

- Fill the two-bidder second-price payoff table; circle the dominant strategy column.
- Play the popup auction with a hidden-value sheet; write the other side's reasoning before your bid.
- Redo Monty Hall assuming a random door opened (sometimes revealing the car); what happens to switching?
- Classify four provided scenarios: dominant strategy exists, or mixed/unpredictable play required.
