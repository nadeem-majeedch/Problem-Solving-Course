Key definitions

Key definitions

  • Strategy — a complete plan of action for every situation that might arise.
  • Dominant strategy — one that is at least as good in every scenario - bid your true value in a second-price auction.
  • Information set — what a decision-maker knows (and does not) at decision time; the host's knowledge changes everything.
  • Best response — the optimal move given what others do - the one-level-up thinking the auction case trains.
  • Equilibrium — a profile of strategies where nobody gains by changing alone; introduced informally.
Explain each in one breath; the cases will do the heavy lifting.
Worked example

The Umbrella Decision: expected value with a decision tree

cs-121

Carry an umbrella (mild inconvenience, cost 2) or not. Rain ruins the day (cost 10). Forecast says 30% rain. What does expected cost say, and when does it flip?

p = 0.3
ev_carry = 2
ev_leave = p * 10
flip = 2 / 10
print(ev_carry < ev_leave, flip)

Expected: carry the umbrella (EV 2 vs 3); ranking flips below 20% rain chance

Cost: O(1) per decision; the tree's value is in forcing outcomes OUT of the prose.

Worked example

The Game Show Switch: conditional probability decides

cs-122

Three doors, one prize. You pick door 1; the host opens door 3 (empty). Should you switch to door 2?

stay_wins = switch_wins = 0
for prize in (1, 2, 3):
    if prize == 1:
        stay_wins += 1
    else:
        switch_wins += 1
print(stay_wins, switch_wins)

Expected: switch: wins 2/3 vs staying 1/3

Cost: O(1) enumeration; the simulation version is O(trials) and validates the tally.

Worked example

The Insurance Riddle: why a bad bet is still rational

cs-123

Insurance costs 50; the insured event (probability 1%) costs 4000. EV says decline (-50 vs -40 expected loss). Why do rational people buy, and what decision rule are they using?

ev_buy = -50
ev_skip = 0.01 * -4000
print(ev_buy, ev_skip, ev_buy < ev_skip)

Expected: EV(buy) -50 vs EV(skip) -40: insurance loses on EV, wins on ruin-avoidance

Cost: O(1); the hard part is admitting which rule you chose.

Check yourself

Common misconceptions

  • Playing a fixed move in games that require unpredictability.
  • Assuming everyone sees the same information you do - the host's knowledge changes the game.
  • Bidding above value 'to be safe' - the second-price table shows the exact risk.
  • Treating promises as commitments; only binding actions change incentives.
