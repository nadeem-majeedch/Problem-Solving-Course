Lecture 01 — Thinking in Problems

Learning objectives

  • Decompose a messy requirement into precise subproblems
  • State assumptions and constraints before solving
  • Choose between alternative interpretations of a task

Case-based lecture: you attempt, we discuss, then we reveal.

Today

The plan

  • Four cases: cs-001, cs-002, cs-003, cs-004
  • Each: 5-minute attempt → discussion → reveal → prompt
  • Worked examples in between — ask anything
  • Break mid-lecture · exit question at the end
Key definitions

Key definitions

  • Problem — a goal plus obstacles: what is wanted, and what stands between the given information and that goal.
  • Specification — a restatement of a problem so that two careful readers would produce the same answer for every input.
  • Input — the information a solution is allowed to use. Everything not listed as input does not exist.
  • Output — the exact form of the answer required (a number, a decision, a list) - agreed before solving.
  • Constraint — a limit the solution must respect (time, memory, money, legal rules).
  • Assumption — a statement you accept without proof to make the problem solvable; always written down.
Explain each in one breath; the cases will do the heavy lifting.
Worked example

The coffee card, modelled honestly

cs-001

A cafe card gives every 10th drink free (buy 9, the 10th costs nothing). Price 3.50 per paid drink; the student wants 22 drinks this month. What do they pay?

Expected: (20, 2, 70.0)

Cost: O(wanted) time, O(1) space; a closed form (paid = wanted - wanted // 10) exists and is worth deriving as a check.

Worked example

The elevator: movement vs stops

cs-002

An elevator serves requests in press order: [3, 7, 3, 1, 5]. 3 s per floor moved, 6 s per stop (including the first). Total time?

Expected: 72

Cost: O(n) time for n requests; the *optimisation* of ordering is a different (harder) problem, previewed in Block III.

Worked example

Which vending machine? Writing the rules

cs-003

Machine A sells at 2.00, exact coins only. Machine B sells at 2.30 and gives change. The student holds specific coins. Write decision rules covering exact match, overpay possible, overpay impossible, and B sold out.

Expected: machine B / machine A / no purchase (per the three traces)

Cost: Constant time; the difficulty is specification completeness, not cost.

Worked example

The library fine, one ambiguity at a time

cs-004

Fines: 0.50/day for days 1-7, 1.00/day for days 8-30, cap 10.00. Returned on day 5, day 12, day 40 - and on the due date itself?

wanted = 22
price = 3.50
paid = wanted - wanted // 10
free = wanted // 10
print(paid, free, paid * price)

Expected: 2.50; 8.50 (our reading); 10.00; due-date return 0.00

Cost: Constant time. The pedagogical content is the two contested readings, not the arithmetic.

Case attempt · lecture 01
Case cs-001 · Beginner

The Coffee Card

A cafe loyalty card: buy 9 drinks, get 1 free. Given the price of a drink and how many drinks a student wants over a month, how much will they actually pay? Must decide what happens when a free drink is 'earned' mid-month.

  • **Input:** price 3.50 per drink; the student wants 22 drinks this month
  • **Output:** 20 paid drinks, total 70.00 (2 free drinks earned: the 10th and the 20th drink)
  • Solve for the worked instance first. Then ask what would change if one
  • number, name, or condition were replaced by something stranger.

  • Which assumption shaped your answer?

The Elevator Debate

Case cs-003 · Intermediate

Two Vending Machines

Two machines sell the same drink for different prices; one accepts exact coins only, the other gives change. Write precise rules for which machine a student should use given the coins they hold.

  • **Output:** exact-coin machine when coins match a price; change machine otherwise; never the exact machine if it means overpaying

Case cs-004 · Expert

The Library Fine Formula

A library charges fines with a tiered rule (per day, capped, and waived under conditions). Turn a vague policy into an exact formula, including what happens on the return day itself.

  • **Output:** 2.50; 7.00 (5 charged days: 7+5x1.00); 10.00 (capped); same-day return 0.00

Check yourself

Common misconceptions

  • Reading the problem once and starting to write - the spec phase is the solution.
  • Treating assumptions as facts: 'of course returns include the due date' - says who?
  • Confusing an example with a definition (one worked instance does not define the rule).
  • Choosing a model silently, then defending the arithmetic instead of the model.
Summary

Summary & exit question

  • Named technique today: Thinking in Problems

Exit question (choose one, 60 seconds)

  2. Give one example of two defensible readings of the same rule.
